    Academic ethics¶

    This chapter answers a single question: how should I treat this person, or this situation?

    You cannot spend years inside an organisation this size without hitting conflict. What follows will not prevent that. It is meant to leave you better prepared when it happens. Adopting any of it is a personal decision — but it is worth reading before you need it rather than after.

    The shape of the place¶

    Think of a university as a large guild of scholars, with everything good and bad that implies. There is genuine care here, something close to family in a good research group, and real generosity with time. There are also people with their own deadlines, their own funding worries and their own goals, which occasionally collide with yours. Both halves are true at once. Expecting only the first leads to disappointment; expecting only the second makes you unpleasant to work with.

    Asking well¶

    Asking costs nothing but reputation, and reputation is the currency here. Learn to tell which questions are cheap and which are expensive, and route them accordingly.

    Use this ladder, in order:

    1. Search. The handbook, the course page, the repository README, the documentation, the internet. A surprising fraction of questions die here.
    2. Ask your peers. Your group, your year, the students who took the course last year.
    3. Ask in the course or School chat. Public questions have a multiplier: everyone else with the same question gets the answer too, and it stays searchable.
    4. Ask the instructor or the person responsible.
    5. Ask the School office.

    To be filled in by the School

    Name the actual channels here — the School's student chat, the course chat convention, the office email address and its response window. Until this is written down, step 3 and step 5 do not really exist.

    The point of the ladder is not to protect senior people from students. It is to make sure that when you do reach them, the question is worth the interruption — and it will get a much better answer for the same reason.

    With that said, do not be afraid of vertical communication. Nobody in this School, up to and including the Dean, will refuse a substantive question. The ladder is about the quality of the question, not the rank of the person.

    Giving people time¶

    If you want a considered answer, leave room for one to be written.

    • Do not send an email and then chase it in a messenger twenty minutes later, then again on social media that evening. You are not the centre of anyone's day; that day contains a lecture, a supervision, a broken instrument and two deadlines.
    • Value the quality of communication, not only its speed. Some answers should take three days.
    • Do not scatter one conversation across four channels. Nobody can reconstruct it later, including you.
    • Watch the clock. People differ in how they feel about a message at one in the morning, and you usually cannot predict which kind of person you are writing to. Default to business hours — roughly not before 09:00 and not after 22:00 — reply within a working day where a reply is expected, and do not write to someone's personal social media account unless they have offered it.

    Conflict¶

    • Never give unsolicited advice after the fact. Once decisions are made and actions taken, "you should have done it this way" is not help. If someone explicitly asks what went wrong, answer fully and honestly — that is a different conversation, and a valuable one.
    • In a disagreement, leave the other person a way to explain themselves and a way out with their dignity intact. People who are cornered stop reasoning and start defending. Sooner or later this will be done for you, too.
    • Separate the work from the person. "This design will fail under load, here is the calculation" is a technical statement. "You clearly do not understand mechanics" is not, and it is also less useful.
    • Assume the boring explanation first. An unanswered message is far more likely to be a full inbox than contempt.

    Instructors¶

    • An instructor is not an encyclopaedia and not infallible. Their job is to build a working educational process, not to have every answer immediately available. "I do not know, let us look it up" is a sign of a good instructor, not a weak one.
    • Most instructors here are also researchers and engineers. Teaching is one of several things they are accountable for. If a course seems to be running below its potential, the cause is usually a stack of grant deadlines and not indifference. Before escalating, try a direct conversation — it resolves most of these.
    • Feedback is welcome and complaints are legitimate, but they work in that order: talk to the instructor first, then the programme lead, then the School. Skipping straight to the top is occasionally necessary and always expensive.

    Credit and honesty¶

    This is the part of academic ethics with formal consequences attached, so it is worth being blunt.

    • Present as your own only what you actually did. This covers coursework, code, figures, measurements and text. Submitting someone else's work as yours is fraud, and it is treated as fraud.
    • Cite sources, including code. Using a Stack Overflow answer, a library, a published algorithm or a classmate's suggestion is normal engineering. Not saying so is not.
    • Report data honestly. Do not delete the run that disagrees with you. Do not smooth a curve into agreement with theory. An experiment that failed, reported clearly, is a contribution; a result that was quietly improved is a liability that will outlive your degree.
    • Give co-authorship and credit where they are due, including to students, technicians and anyone whose work you depend on. Agree the authorship order before the paper is written, not after.
    • Do not cover for a colleague's fabrication. It is the one loyalty this place cannot extend to you.

    Rules for AI tools are the same rules applied to a new instrument. They are in Working with AI tools.

    Asking for things¶

    Show what you can do before you ask for preferences. A request to join a research group, to be lent an instrument, to be recommended for a programme or to be given an exception lands very differently when it arrives attached to evidence. A repository, a working prototype, a report, a completed course — anything demonstrable is worth more than a paragraph of enthusiasm.

    This is not gatekeeping. It is the fastest way we have of telling that you are serious, and it is entirely within your power to produce.

    Money¶

    Payment in a university does not work like payment in a company. Outside the administrative staff, very few people receive one salary once a month. Income is assembled from scholarships, teaching and research assistantships, grants, prizes, competitions and one-off supplements — and it arrives irregularly, often as a large disbursement a few times a year with thin months in between.

    Two consequences. Learn to plan a budget across an uneven year; the first time a grant instalment slips by a month should not be a crisis. And understand that in research, "did the work, got paid" is not the whole mechanism — a good deal of what is done here is done out of curiosity, and the people who work that way tend to be the ones the money eventually follows.
