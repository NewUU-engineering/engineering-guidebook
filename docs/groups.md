# Small groups and projects

[Cooperation](orientations.md#cooperation) is one of our orientations. This chapter is the practical half of it.

## Why groups

The small group suits us because it matches the actual shape of the work. Almost everything a person faces at a university — a course project, a paper, a purchase order, a broken rig, a deadline — is handled better by three people than by one. Members reinforce each other's competences and cover each other's gaps, and experience transfers between them far faster and far more naturally than it transfers through a lecture.

We do not prescribe how a group is organised. It can be students only, staff only, both, or people from other universities and other schools. Its internal structure, division of responsibility and working rules are entirely up to its members.

## What the School will support

One condition attaches to active School support:

> **The group's main goal must be connected to science, education or technology, and its work must be presented publicly.**

If a climbing club asks the School to fund a trip, that is not our business. If a group studying anchor failure under cyclic loading asks to go and instrument some real climbing hardware, that is exactly our business. The same logic applies to everything else, including rest: if a project team decides that two days away from the lab will make the next month better, that is a reasonable thing to ask for and a reasonable thing to grant.

Support means, concretely:

- **Space and equipment.** The School has laboratories — robotics, materials, physics, acoustics, wireless — with instruments in them. What stands between you and access is convincing someone that your intentions are serious and your safety practice is sound. See [Safety is part of the culture](safety.md).
- **Media reach.** We can publicise your project, put a call for members in front of every student in the School, or arrange an expert to tear your idea apart before you spend a semester on it. Ask.
- **Introductions.** To staff, to other groups, to the School of Computing, to industry contacts, to people at partner universities.
- **Money, sometimes.** Internal competitions, university grant schemes and external calls all exist. The path to them runs through your supervisor and the School office.

## For the people who prefer to work alone

The group format tends to meet resistance from people who work best on their own, and the objection deserves a straight answer.

Total freedom over how the group is organised is precisely what makes it survivable for individualists. Modern technical work — especially research — is overwhelmingly produced by coordinated teams, and that is not a fashion, it is a consequence of how much a serious result now costs. Adapting to that is not optional. The small group is the gentlest available way to do it: you choose the people, you divide the work to suit your own preferences, and if what you want is to grind away at one hard problem alone and hand results over periodically, that is a perfectly good role inside a group.

## Do not close in on yourselves

The failure mode of a good small group is becoming a sealed room. Six months of enjoyable work, no external contact, and a result nobody wants.

The defence is feedback obtained in public: papers, a blog, videos, seminars, conferences, hackathons, a repository people actually visit. That is the only way to find out whether your strategy is right and your plan needs correcting. It is also how the School finds out that your group is alive, which turns out to matter when you ask for something.

If you do not know where to start, start where the constraints are real. Some directions with genuine depth, and genuine demand, in this region:

|---|---|
| **Water and environment** | Irrigation efficiency and losses, soil salinity, dust and aerosol transport from the dried Aral bed, air quality monitoring in Tashkent |
| **Energy** | Solar in a continental climate, storage and thermal management, grid stability, efficiency retrofits for existing industrial plant |
| **Materials and chemistry** | Local mineral and metallurgical feedstocks, catalysts, polymers and composites, corrosion, cement and construction materials |
| **Robotics and automation** | Agricultural and warehouse automation, mobile robots, manipulation, inspection of infrastructure, teaching platforms |
| **Aerospace and instrumentation** | UAV design and control, remote sensing for agriculture, small-satellite payloads, sensor development |
| **Computational physics and modelling** | Simulation of processes above, materials modelling, data assimilation, machine learning applied to physical systems |

You will notice the same feature everywhere: engineering now sits at the junction of several fields at once. Expect your project to need someone who is not from your department.

## Getting a project started

There is no formula — how a project starts depends on who is starting it — but a few things reliably help.

- **Recruit while you can.** Almost nobody notices this at the time: your years at university are practically the only period in your life with several hundred capable, available people around you whom you can approach directly and cheaply. Start with your own year, then the School, then the university, then other universities. This window closes quietly.
- **Be able to explain the project in ninety seconds.** The formats that work: a clear README in a public repository, a pitch deck, a two-minute video. If you cannot explain it in ninety seconds, you have not finished thinking about it.
- **Use the School's physical resources.** Rooms, laboratories, instruments, computing. They exist to be used by people who will use them properly.
- **Use the School's media resources.** Ask us to advertise you, to find you members, to get you reviewed.
- **If you would rather join than lead, say so.** Send us what you can do and what you want to learn. Groups are constantly short of the person who can do the thing you can do.
- **Go after money early.** Internal competitions and grant calls have deadlines that arrive long before you feel ready. Applying and losing costs a weekend and teaches you how the process works.

## Running the group

A short list of things that separate groups that finish from groups that dissolve.

1. **Write down who owns what.** Not a hierarchy — an owner per area, so that no task belongs to "everyone".
2. **Keep the work in one place, in the open.** One repository, one issue tracker, one document. Decisions made in a private message do not exist.
3. **Meet on a fixed rhythm**, briefly, and always with the state of the work as the first item.
4. **Write things down as you go.** The lab notebook, the README, the parameter that took two days to find. Your future self is a different person with no memory of this week.
5. **Publish something every few months**, even if it is small. A group that has not shown anything in a semester is usually stuck and does not know it yet.
6. **Plan the handover.** People graduate. A project that cannot survive its founder leaving was a hobby, not a project.
