# Passing a course

Almost everything that determines your grade is decided in the first two weeks of the semester, and almost nobody acts as though that were true.

## Week one

**Establish contact with the instructor.** Get a working email address and make sure a course chat exists. If none exists, propose one — a student may create it, and most instructors will be glad someone did. Where you cannot reach an instructor directly, the School office can put you in contact.

**Ask how the grade is composed.** Precisely: how many points come from what, what the deadlines are, what late work costs, what the resit rules are. Then ask the two questions that actually matter:

- What does the *best* possible run through this course look like?
- What does the *worst* look like, and at what point does it become unrecoverable?

An instructor who has thought about their course can answer both in a minute. The answers tell you where to spend effort, which is the whole game.

**Ask what is assumed.** Every course assumes prior material. Find out which, and check honestly whether you have it. Two weeks of quiet remediation in September is cheaper than a failed midterm in November.

**Ask about tools.** Which software, which licences, whether there is a free alternative you can run on your own laptop. If a required package is paid or restricted, raise it early — the School would rather find a licence or a substitute than discover in week ten that half the cohort cannot do the assignment.

## Staying in contact

**Keeping the channel open is the student's responsibility, not the instructor's.** This is not unfair; it is arithmetic. One instructor cannot chase sixty students, and a system that expects them to will collapse into chasing nobody.

Reasonable expectations, absent anything the instructor has said themselves:

| Channel | Use it for | Expect a reply in |
|---|---|---|
| **Email** | Long or complicated questions, anything about grades, anything you may need to refer back to | A few days |
| **Course chat** | Coordination, short clarifications, "which room" | Within the working day |
| **Office hours** | Anything you have already tried to solve and could not | Immediately, in person |
| **School office** | Urgent matters and unreachable instructors | Per office policy |

Do not run long technical arguments in a chat. If a discussion needs structure, open a shared document, put the points in it, and bring the document.

## Working the course

- **Show up even when you have prepared nothing.** Skipping a class you are unready for is the single most expensive habit available to a student: you lose the material, the context and the contact all at once, and you make the next class harder still. Attend, say plainly that you did not finish, and use the time.
- **Use office hours.** They are almost always empty. An hour there is worth several hours alone with a textbook, and it is the cheapest way to become someone the instructor knows by name.
- **Find a second source and compare it.** One textbook gives you one author's way of seeing the topic; two give you a way of noticing when an explanation is weak. Where the course book is a standard English text, a Russian-language treatment of the same subject is often a genuinely different approach rather than a translation — and if you can read both, use both.
- **Look for the same course elsewhere.** [MIT OpenCourseWare](https://ocw.mit.edu/), [edX](https://www.edx.org/), [Coursera](https://www.coursera.org/), [Khan Academy](https://www.khanacademy.org/), and for the mathematics and physics of it, [3Blue1Brown](https://www.3blue1brown.com/). Ask your instructor whether completing a specific external course can earn points or replace part of the assessment — some will say yes, and none will mind being asked.
- **Prepare before laboratory sessions.** Lab time is the scarcest resource in an engineering degree, and it is not returned to you. Arriving having read the procedure is the difference between doing an experiment and watching one.
- **Test yourself instead of re-reading.** Re-reading notes produces a strong feeling of familiarity and very little retention. Closing the book and reconstructing the derivation from memory is unpleasant, which is exactly why it works. Past papers and worked problems beat highlighters.
- **Work with other people, and stay on the right side of the line.** Studying together, arguing about a problem and explaining a concept to a classmate are all legitimate and all effective. Copying their solution is not. If you are unsure where a specific course draws the line, ask the instructor in writing.

## Language

Instruction here is in English, and by the time you graduate your written English should be good enough to publish in. Treat it as an engineering skill and not a subject you already passed: the technical literature, the documentation, the standards, the error messages and the job market all run on it, and translating in your head is a tax you pay on every one of them.

That is the floor. On top of it, this region gives you Uzbek and Russian for free, and a very large body of technical work — textbooks, standards, decades of engineering practice — exists in Russian and nowhere else. Reading two technical traditions is a real professional advantage, and few of your future competitors will have it.

## When a course is not working

Sometimes the problem is not you. Handle it in this order:

1. **Talk to the instructor directly**, early, and about the specific problem — pace, an unclear assessment rule, a broken assumption. Most cases end here.
2. **Talk to your programme lead** if that does not resolve it.
3. **Take it to the School office** if it is still unresolved or is not something you can raise with the instructor.

Do this while the semester can still be changed. Feedback delivered in the end-of-course survey is useful to next year's students and useless to you.
